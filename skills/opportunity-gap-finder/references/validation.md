# Validation

## Summary

- Argue against each lead opportunity before recommending commitment.
- Choose the cheapest safe experiment that tests the riskiest assumption.

## Phase 9: Stress-Test the Opportunity

For each leading opportunity, argue against it.

Ask:

- Why might users tolerate the current pain?
- Why has this not already been solved?
- What failed before, and why?
- What hidden constraint may make the workaround rational?
- Is the problem severe only for a tiny group?
- Is the proposed solution solving a symptom?
- Could an incumbent, policy change, local condition, or platform make this obsolete?
- Is the behaviour temporary?
- Are people complaining but unwilling to act?
- Could the solution increase complexity?
- Who could be harmed, excluded, displaced, manipulated, or burdened?
- Does the opportunity still fit the user's success threshold and desired role?
- What must be true for this opportunity to work?
- What evidence would disprove it?

Record each critical assumption:

```text
Assumption:
Why it matters:
Current evidence:
Test:
Success threshold:
Failure threshold:
Safety or ethical boundary:
```

## Phase 10: Select Validation Experiments

Do not recommend the full commitment first.

Choose the cheapest safe, reversible experiment that tests the riskiest assumption at the required evidence level.

Possible experiments:

- Interviews
- Observation or workflow shadowing
- Complaint analysis
- Manual concierge service
- Prototype
- Clickable mock-up
- Landing page
- Waitlist
- Paid pilot
- Pre-order
- Tutorial
- Open-source utility
- Spreadsheet or template
- Community session
- Integration test
- Pricing conversation
- Behavioural diary
- A/B test
- Search-demand analysis
- Pop-up, workshop, event, or temporary installation
- Small-batch or limited-area delivery
- Partnership or permission test
- Grant, sponsor, or volunteer commitment test
- Field observation
- Expert, legal, safety, or ethics review

Each validation experiment must include:

```text
Hypothesis:
Riskiest assumption:
Target participant:
Experiment:
Success signal:
Failure signal:
Time or sample boundary:
Maximum downside:
Consent, safety, or ethical safeguards:
Decision after result:
```

Prefer behavioural evidence over compliments.

### Strong signals

- Payment
- Repeated usage
- Data sharing
- Referrals
- Time invested
- Workflow migration
- A signed pilot
- Continued use without reminders
- Users creating their own workarounds
- Users asking when the solution will be available
- Participants returning, inviting others, or changing behaviour
- Partners granting access, resources, permission, or distribution
- Measurable benefit for the intended actor

### Weak signals

- "That sounds cool."
- Likes
- General social engagement
- Friends saying they would use it
- Survey respondents claiming hypothetical willingness to pay
- Participation driven only by incentives that will not exist in normal use
- Broad population statistics with no evidence about the target actor

## Validation Ladder

Increase commitment only when the previous level supports it:

1. **Problem evidence** — the actor, desired progress, consequence, and current behaviour are real.
2. **Alternative gap** — existing options leave a meaningful shortfall.
3. **Mechanism evidence** — the intervention creates the intended result in a small test.
4. **Commitment evidence** — relevant actors contribute money, time, trust, access, permission, or behaviour change.
5. **Repeatability evidence** — value can be delivered more than once without unacceptable cost or unsustainable personal effort.
6. **Sustainability evidence** — economics, labour, governance, maintenance, safety, and incentives can persist.

Stop, reframe, or narrow when a failure threshold is reached. Do not reinterpret every negative result as a reason to continue.
