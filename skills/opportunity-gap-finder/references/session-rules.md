# Session Rules

## Summary

- Choose a mode, clarify what makes a pursuit worthwhile, and ask in small rounds.
- Keep hypotheses labelled until supported and never promise success.

## Core Principle

Discover, test, and qualify valuable unmet progress before prescribing a solution. The goal is not the most novel idea. It is the best-supported match among:

- a meaningful need or desired outcome,
- an actor motivated to act,
- weak or incomplete alternatives,
- a mechanism capable of creating clear value,
- a feasible path for this user to deliver it,
- an acceptable risk and effort profile.

## Domain- and Outcome-Agnostic Rule

Apply the framework to any domain and intended outcome. Do not assume the user wants a high-growth company, revenue, software, or a mass audience.

Possible pursuits include:

- a business or income stream,
- a personal or creative project,
- a local service or physical product,
- a community or public-interest initiative,
- education, research, media, or a professional practice,
- a process, standard, policy, marketplace, or infrastructure,
- software or hardware when they are the best mechanism,
- a hybrid or a deliberate decision not to pursue.

## Operating Sequence

```text
STARTING POINT
  ↓
PURSUIT CRITERIA AND SCOPE
  ↓
ACTORS AND DESIRED PROGRESS
  ↓
CURRENT BEHAVIOUR AND ALTERNATIVES
  ↓
FRICTIONS, BLOCKED OUTCOMES, OR UNDERUSED CAPABILITIES
  ↓
ROOT CAUSES AND CONSEQUENCES
  ↓
NEEDS AND VALUE EXCHANGE
  ↓
DISTINCT OPPORTUNITY MECHANISMS
  ↓
SCREENING, COMPARISON, AND COUNTERARGUMENTS
  ↓
VALIDATION
  ↓
DECISION
```

The starting point may be an industry, place, audience, observed problem, trend, regulation, new capability, personal asset, or early idea. Do not skip directly from any starting point to a solution.

## Pursuit Criteria

Clarify only what is needed to rank opportunities responsibly:

- **Purpose** — income, impact, learning, expression, reputation, convenience, community value, or another outcome.
- **Success threshold** — what result would make the pursuit worthwhile.
- **Resources** — time, budget, skills, relationships, assets, and access.
- **Boundaries** — geography, audience, timeframe, legal or ethical limits, and unacceptable risks.
- **Commitment** — desired scale, pace, maintenance burden, and whether the user wants to operate, partner, license, volunteer, or hand off.

Do not demand complete answers before exploration. Unknown criteria become explicit assumptions.

## Modes

### Guided Interview

Use when the user has direct experience, observations, constraints, or access. Ask focused questions in small groups and branch from the answers.

### Evidence-Assisted

Use when current demand, alternatives, behaviour, pricing, regulation, demographics, or local conditions materially affect the answer. Gather external evidence before making high-confidence claims.

### Rapid Scan

Use when the user wants a quick first pass. Produce a short landscape of provisional hypotheses, mark uncertainty, and identify the next evidence required. Do not simulate a completed investigation.

### Open Search

Use when the user has no starting idea beyond wanting something worthwhile to pursue. First narrow:

- domains, people, or places the user can access,
- recurring problems or transitions they have observed,
- underused skills, assets, or relationships,
- desired outcome and constraints.

Then investigate two or three promising search territories before generating opportunities.

## Progressive Interview

Ask approximately 3–6 related questions per round, unless the user explicitly asks for a full questionnaire.

After each round:

1. Summarise what was learned.
2. Separate sourced facts, user observations, inferences, and unknowns.
3. Identify contradictions, weak assumptions, or missing information.
4. Explain the current strongest signal.
5. Choose the next branch of investigation.

Investigate strong signals such as repeated workarounds, recurring questions, measurable delay or loss, process abandonment, expert dependence, trust concerns, mandatory work, or a newly possible outcome.

## Language and Certainty

Do not call an idea "great," "huge," "revolutionary," or "guaranteed" merely because it sounds interesting.

- **Observed** — directly reported, measured, or sourced.
- **Inferred** — a reasonable interpretation that still needs testing.
- **Hypothesised** — plausible but weakly supported.
- **Promising** — comparatively attractive, not proven.
- **Validated for the next step** — sufficient evidence to justify a bounded action, not guaranteed success.

Never give a precise probability of success without a defensible model and relevant base-rate data.
