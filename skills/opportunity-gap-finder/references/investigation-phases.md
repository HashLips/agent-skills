# Investigation Phases

## Summary

- Run phases 1–8 before ranking.
- Convert any starting point into actors, desired progress, alternatives, frictions, causes, needs, and opportunity mechanisms.

## Phase 1: Frame the Search

Start from an open domain, place, population, observed problem, repeated workaround, trend, regulation, new capability, underused asset, or early idea.

Establish:

- **Search territory** — what is in and out of scope.
- **Pursuit purpose** — what value the user wants to create or receive.
- **Success threshold** — what would make the effort worthwhile.
- **Constraints and assets** — time, budget, capability, access, location, ethics, and risk.
- **Relevant change** — what became newly possible, difficult, expected, costly, compulsory, or acceptable, if anything.
- **Initial assumptions** — what is believed but not yet supported.

Create one or both framing statements:

```text
Search statement:
Find [type of value] opportunities for or with [actors/domain]
within [scope and constraints], where success means [threshold].

Change statement, when relevant:
Because [change], [actor] can or must now [new behaviour or outcome],
which alters [workflow, incentive, risk, access, or expectation].
```

## Phase 2: Map Affected People

Do not treat "the market" or "users" as a homogeneous group.

Identify distinct actors:

- Direct users
- Buyers
- Decision-makers
- Beneficiaries
- Operators
- Administrators
- Experts
- Beginners
- Regulators
- Service providers
- Influencers and gatekeepers
- People negatively affected by the change

For each group, capture:

- Desired outcome
- Current alternative, including doing nothing
- Current workflow
- Existing skill level
- Constraints
- Incentives
- Fears
- Ability to pay
- Authority to adopt a solution

Use one actor record per meaningful segment:

```text
Actor:
Desired progress:
Current behaviour or alternative:
Main constraint:
Consequence of failure:
Beneficiary:
Decision-maker:
Who contributes money, time, permission, or trust:
Access path:
```

Prioritise the actor with the strongest combination of consequence, motivation, accessibility, and fit with the user's pursuit criteria.

## Phase 3: Understand Current Behaviour

Investigate what people actually do, not only what they say they want.

Questions:

- What outcome are they trying to achieve?
- What triggers the effort?
- What steps, people, tools, places, or decisions are involved?
- What do they use, hire, learn, combine, postpone, or avoid today?
- Where do they spend money, time, attention, reputation, or political capital?
- Why might the current approach be rational despite complaints?
- What are they doing now that they did not do before?
- What are they doing more frequently?
- What are they doing less frequently?
- What new tools or workarounds have they adopted?
- What new jobs, roles, communities, or vocabulary have appeared?
- What is now expected of them?
- What have they stopped paying for?
- What tasks have shifted from experts to beginners?
- What tasks moved from organisations to individuals, or vice versa?
- What decisions are now made faster or with less certainty?

Classify each behaviour as:

- New behaviour
- Increased behaviour
- Reduced behaviour
- Forced behaviour
- Workaround behaviour
- Status or identity behaviour
- Risk-avoidance behaviour
- Non-consumption or avoidance
- Delegation or expert reliance

## Phase 4: Detect Frictions, Blocked Outcomes, and Latent Demand

Investigate both pain and possibility.

### Friction signals

Look for:

- Repetition
- Waiting
- Confusion
- Errors
- Rework
- Manual transfer
- Tool switching
- Lost context
- Fragmented information
- High cost
- Expert dependency
- Compliance burden
- Fear
- Lack of trust
- Poor onboarding
- Difficult deployment
- Lack of interoperability
- Poor accessibility
- Addictive or unhealthy usage patterns
- Too many choices
- Unclear ownership
- Unclear return on effort
- Work that users repeatedly postpone
- Steps where people abandon the process

### Blocked-progress signals

Look for:

- People wanting an outcome they cannot yet achieve
- A capability available to experts but not ordinary users
- A new technology without usable workflows
- Repeated custom scripts or spreadsheets
- Communities rebuilding the same solution independently
- A process that is technically possible but not productised
- A result that requires too many tools
- A tool that exists but is too expensive, complex, or narrow
- Users combining several products to approximate one desired outcome
- People excluded by location, language, disability, income, trust, or eligibility
- An outcome valued for identity, joy, belonging, status, or expression rather than efficiency
- A desirable outcome with no obvious buyer but a credible sponsor, institution, or collective-benefit path

For each friction, record:

```text
Actor:
Behaviour:
Observed friction:
Evidence:
Current workaround:
Consequence:
Frequency:
Severity:
Root-cause hypothesis:
Confidence:
```

## Phase 5: Map Alternatives and the Unserved Gap

Compare:

- direct solutions,
- indirect substitutes,
- professional or informal services,
- manual workarounds,
- internal processes,
- non-consumption, delay, or acceptance of the problem,
- prior solutions that failed, disappeared, or remained niche.

For each important alternative, capture:

```text
Alternative:
Who chooses it:
Progress it enables:
Why it is chosen:
Where it fails:
Cost or trade-off:
Switching barrier:
Evidence:
```

Do not call a crowded field "saturated" without checking whether a specific actor, context, outcome, delivery model, trust requirement, or price point remains underserved. Do not call a field "open" merely because no direct competitor was found; absence may indicate weak demand or difficult economics.

## Phase 6: Find the Root Cause

Do not solve the visible symptom too quickly.

Use repeated "why" questions and classify the root cause.

Common root-cause categories:

- Missing knowledge
- Missing capability
- Poor interface
- Fragmented workflow
- Incentive misalignment
- Lack of trust
- High switching cost
- Missing standard
- Regulation
- Cost
- Technical limitation
- Social resistance
- Habit
- Coordination failure
- Information overload
- Ownership ambiguity
- Poor distribution
- Weak feedback loop
- Lack of proof
- Lack of support

Check whether the cause is:

- solvable by the user or a partner,
- structural but addressable through a different mechanism,
- outside practical influence,
- a symptom of incentives that would resist the proposed intervention.

## Phase 7: Convert Evidence Into a Need Statement

A good need statement describes the outcome without prescribing the product.

Use:

```text
[Actor] needs a way to [desired progress]
without [main friction or unacceptable trade-off]
because [important consequence].
```

Reject need statements that are:

- Too broad
- Product-specific
- Based only on the pursuer's preference
- Unsupported by a real actor
- Detached from a consequence
- Merely fashionable

For non-commercial or intrinsic pursuits, the consequence may be missed participation, reduced wellbeing, exclusion, lost expression, weak community capacity, or unrealised learning. Do not force every need into financial language.

## Phase 8: Generate Opportunity Mechanisms

Generate multiple mechanisms, not merely multiple versions of the same app.

For each need, consider:

- Remove the step
- Automate the step
- Compress the step
- Teach the step
- Standardise the step
- Make the step observable
- Make the step trustworthy
- Transfer the step to an expert
- Coordinate multiple actors
- Package an existing expert workflow
- Create infrastructure
- Create a marketplace
- Create a managed service
- Create a community
- Create a physical tool
- Create a behavioural intervention
- Create a policy or governance mechanism
- Adapt an existing solution for a neglected context
- Curate, translate, certify, repair, maintain, or operate
- Produce research, art, media, events, or shared spaces
- Combine mechanisms when the dependency is essential

For every direction, state:

```text
Opportunity mechanism:
Primary actor:
Need and value created:
Why current alternatives fall short:
Behaviour required:
Value exchange or sustaining model:
Key dependency:
Main risk:
Fit with the user's criteria:
```

Keep mechanisms materially distinct. Avoid naming or branding a solution before understanding how it creates and sustains value.
